04 · CLOUD ECONOMICS · USAGE-BASED EGRESS

Turning infrastructure cost into product behavior.

How I defined the metering, attribution and commercialization model needed to make cloud data egress measurable and billable.

01 · The problem

Infrastructure cost did not yet have customer-facing semantics.

Data could move across platform boundaries in several ways. Before egress could become a customer-facing model, we needed to define what movement counted, who received the usage, and what telemetry downstream billing could rely on.

PRODUCT QUESTIONHow do we turn cloud-provider economics into usage customers can understand and the platform can reliably measure?

I moved to SIMS before the full implementation was delivered, so my contribution was product definition and strategy rather than end-to-end launch.

04 · Key product decisions

The judgment calls behind the pricing model.

Commercializing egress meant making decisions at two layers: how customers should be priced, and how usage should be attributed when data moved through shared workflows.

DECISION 01Follow AWS pricing while creating a sustainable margin.+

Customers were already familiar with AWS data-transfer pricing, so we wanted the model to feel recognizable rather than inventing an entirely new way to charge for egress. At the same time, simply passing through our aggregate AWS cost would not create a sustainable commercial model.

THE TRADEOFF

A simple flat rate could create more margin, but it would be less aligned with the tiered pricing customers already understood from AWS. Passing through our exact aggregate AWS cost would be harder to attribute fairly because multiple customers shared the same underlying cloud account.

THE DECISION

Apply AWS-style internet-transfer tiers independently to each customer's usage. Customers received a familiar tiered pricing model, while restarting the tiers at the customer level created margin above the aggregate AWS cost.

DECISION 02Charge the customer who initiates the egress.+

Shared data made billing ownership ambiguous. A customer could download data they did not originally own, and project or delegated workflows could involve multiple users and billing contexts.

THE TRADEOFF

Charging the data owner could make someone pay for a transfer they did not request. Trying to infer ownership from every project or sharing relationship would make the billing model harder to explain and implement consistently.

THE DECISION

Attribute egress to the tenant of the user who initiates the transfer. For the MVP, that rule stays consistent even when data is shared or project billing is involved, making the charge traceable to the action that created it.
