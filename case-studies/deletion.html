05 · Data lifecycle

Designing deletion when data has dependencies.

A technical product case study in lifecycle state, asynchronous operations, retention and recovery.

RoleTechnical Product Manager
ProblemDeleting data could affect linked and shared resources
GoalCreate safer, recoverable lifecycle behavior
FocusDependencies, retention, recovery and purge

The problem

What made this difficult.

In a data platform, delete is rarely one database operation. Analyses, projects, files and shared resources can depend on one another, and large operations may be asynchronous. A destructive action therefore needed explicit lifecycle behavior rather than a simple permanent-delete button.

Key product decisions

The decisions underneath the feature.

01

Treat delete as a lifecycle

The model separated the user's delete request from irreversible purge so data could enter a recoverable state first.

02

Respect dependencies

The product needed rules for resources that were linked, shared or required by another object instead of silently breaking downstream workflows.

03

Design for asynchronous execution

Large data operations may not finish immediately. Product behavior had to account for in-progress states, ordering and what users could do while deletion was underway.

04

Make recovery a first-class path

Retention only helps if recovery semantics are clear. The design considered the window in which data could be restored and the point after which purge became irreversible.

My role & impact

Where I contributed.

I explored the product requirements and lifecycle model with engineering, translating a broad request for deletion into states, dependency behavior, retention and recovery considerations. As with the other cloud work, I moved to SIMS before the complete implementation was delivered.

Technical deep dive

What I had to understand to make the product decisions.

The conceptual flow became: request deletion, hide or mark the resource, prevent incompatible dependent operations, retain for a defined period, then either recover or permanently purge. The value of the model was making each transition and its consequences explicit.

What I learned

Destructive actions deserve as much product design as creation flows. The difficult part is often not the endpoint itself but the states, dependencies and failure modes around it.
