05 · DATA LIFECYCLE · RECOVERABLE DELETION

Designing deletion when data has dependencies.

How I helped turn a destructive action into an explicit lifecycle model spanning dependencies, asynchronous states, retention, recovery and purge.

RoleTechnical Product Manager
StageProduct definition
EnvironmentEnterprise data platform
FocusLifecycle · dependencies · recovery

01 · The problem

Delete was not one database operation.

Analyses, projects, files and shared resources could depend on one another, while large operations could complete asynchronously. A simple permanent-delete action risked breaking downstream workflows or creating unclear system states.

PRODUCT QUESTIONHow should deletion behave when data is connected, shared and too large to disappear instantly?

02 · My role & impact

I translated a broad deletion request into explicit lifecycle behavior.

I explored the requirements and lifecycle model with engineering, working through states, dependencies, retention, recovery and failure considerations. I moved to SIMS before the complete implementation was delivered.

04 · Key product decisions

The tradeoffs that shaped the work.

DECISION 01Separate delete from purge.+

Immediate irreversible deletion left no room for recovery or asynchronous platform behavior.

THE DECISION

Model deletion as a state transition with a recoverable period before permanent purge.

DECISION 02Respect dependencies before removing data.+

Resources could be linked, shared or required by another object.

THE DECISION

Define behavior for dependent resources instead of allowing deletion to silently break workflows.

DECISION 03Make recovery a first-class path.+

Retention is only useful when users and systems know what can be restored and when.

THE DECISION

Define recovery semantics alongside deletion and the eventual irreversible state.

05 · Skills & takeaways

What deletion taught me about lifecycle product design.

01State modeling

Destructive actions need explicit states and transitions just as much as creation workflows.

02Edge-case thinking

Dependencies and failure modes often contain the hardest product decisions.

03Async systems

Large operations require product behavior for in-progress states, not just success and failure.

04Technical judgment

A simple customer request can imply deep architectural and lifecycle consequences.
