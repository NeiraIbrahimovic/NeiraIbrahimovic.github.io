06 · MULTI-TENANT COMPUTE · FAIR SCHEDULING

Making sure one large customer can’t take over the queue.

How we designed fair access to shared compute so high-volume customers could not crowd smaller customers out of the queue.

RoleTechnical Product Manager contributor
StageProduct exploration
EnvironmentShared batch processing
FocusQueueing · QoS · allocation

01 · The problem

A few large customers could consume most of the shared compute.

Some customers submitted far more work than others. Under a simple queue, those high-volume tenants could fill available capacity and leave smaller customers waiting behind a backlog they did not create. We needed a scheduling model that gave every customer a meaningful opportunity to run work while still using compute efficiently.

PRODUCT QUESTIONHow do we stop high-volume customers from crowding smaller customers out of shared compute?

02 · My role & impact

I helped shape the product thinking around scheduler behavior.

I worked through fair queueing, quality-of-service priorities, round-robin scheduling and token-based allocation to define how shared compute should be distributed across customers.

04 · Key product decisions

The judgment calls behind fair access.

Fair scheduling was not just an algorithm choice. We had to decide how capacity should rotate between customers and whether customers should have a way to pay for differentiated priority.

DECISION 01Use round-robin scheduling so large customers could not dominate the queue.+

A first-come, first-served queue rewarded whoever submitted the most work first. That meant a large customer could build a deep backlog and force smaller customers to wait behind it, even when they only had a small amount of work to run.

THE TRADEOFF

Strict arrival order was simple and predictable, but it did not create fair access across customers with very different compute volumes. A fairer model needed to distribute opportunities across tenants rather than only across individual jobs.

THE DECISION

Use round-robin scheduling across customer queues so each tenant receives a turn before the scheduler cycles back. Large customers could still process significant workloads, but their backlog could no longer completely crowd smaller customers out.

DECISION 02Offer higher queue priority as a paid service level.+

Not every workload had the same urgency. Some customers valued faster access to compute enough to pay for it, creating an opportunity to connect scheduler priority with a differentiated commercial offering.

THE TRADEOFF

Giving every workload identical priority preserved a simple fairness model, but offered no way to serve customers with more time-sensitive needs. Unlimited paid priority, however, could recreate the same starvation problem fair queueing was designed to solve.

THE DECISION

Create a higher-priority service level customers could pay for, while preserving fair scheduling rules so priority improved a customer’s position without allowing one tenant to monopolize shared compute.
