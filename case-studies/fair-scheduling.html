06 · MULTI-TENANT COMPUTE · FAIR SCHEDULING

Fair does not always mean first come, first served.

A distributed-systems product case study in shared compute, queue fairness, QoS and resource allocation.

RoleTechnical Product Manager contributor
StageProduct exploration
EnvironmentShared batch processing
FocusQueueing · QoS · allocation

01 · The problem

Multiple tenants were competing for finite compute.

Pure first-come, first-served scheduling can let one tenant or workload consume disproportionate capacity. The platform needed rules that balanced fairness, priority and predictable access to shared resources.

PRODUCT QUESTIONWhat should fair mean when different customers and workloads compete for the same compute?

02 · My role & impact

I contributed to the product thinking around scheduler behavior.

I contributed to work around fair queueing, quality-of-service priorities, round-robin scheduling and token-based allocation. I did not lead this workstream through delivery. I include it because it deepened how I reason about multi-tenant platform behavior.

04 · Key product decisions

The tradeoffs that shaped the work.

DECISION 01Define who receives fairness.+

Fairness is ambiguous unless the system knows whether it applies to a user, tenant, queue or workload.

THE DECISION

Make the unit of fairness explicit before choosing scheduling behavior.

DECISION 02Priority cannot mean monopoly.+

Some workloads need differentiated service, but priority should not let one tenant consume the platform indefinitely.

THE DECISION

Balance QoS with safeguards that preserve access for competing tenants.

DECISION 03Product language must match the scheduler.+

Customers experience the consequences of infrastructure policy even when they never see the algorithm.

THE DECISION

Use terms like fairness and priority only when the underlying behavior consistently supports them.
