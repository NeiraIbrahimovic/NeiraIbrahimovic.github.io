06 · Multi-tenant compute

Fair does not always mean first come, first served.

A distributed-systems product case study in shared compute, queue fairness, QoS and resource allocation.

RoleTechnical Product Manager contributor
ProblemMultiple tenants and workloads competed for finite compute
GoalBalance fairness with workload priority
FocusQueueing, QoS and allocation

The problem

What made this difficult.

A shared compute platform has to decide what happens when demand exceeds available capacity. Pure first-come, first-served behavior can let one tenant dominate the queue, while strict priority can starve lower-priority work. The product problem was defining what fair behavior should mean across customers and workload types.

Key product decisions

The decisions underneath the feature.

01

Fairness needed more than arrival time

Queue behavior had to consider tenant-level fairness rather than allowing a burst from one customer to monopolize capacity.

02

Priority still mattered

Some workloads could warrant higher quality-of-service treatment, so fairness and priority needed to coexist rather than being treated as opposites.

03

Allocation needed explicit rules

Round-robin concepts and token-based allocation provided ways to distribute opportunity across competing tenants while respecting constraints.

04

Product semantics had to match scheduler behavior

Terms such as priority and fairness only help customers if the underlying scheduling behavior consistently reflects them.

My role & impact

Where I contributed.

I contributed to product work around fair queueing, QoS, round-robin scheduling and token-based allocation. I did not lead this workstream through delivery. I include it because it materially deepened my understanding of multi-tenant platform behavior and the product decisions embedded inside distributed systems.

Technical deep dive

What I had to understand to make the product decisions.

The interesting product layer sat above the scheduling algorithms: which entity receives fairness, how priority is represented, how capacity is allocated over time, and how the system avoids one tenant consuming disproportionate resources.

What I learned

Infrastructure policies are product policies. Even when customers never see the scheduler, its rules determine wait time, predictability and whether a shared platform feels fair.
