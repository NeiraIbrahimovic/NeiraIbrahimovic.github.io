01

0→1 enterprise data platform

So... what exactly is a sample?

This turned out to be a surprisingly difficult question. Different products had different answers. The same biological sample could become disconnected as it moved through a workflow. Customers learned to stitch the story back together themselves.

Usually with spreadsheets. Lots of spreadsheets.

I owned SIMS from an early concept into a real product. That meant figuring out what the product should be, shaping the data model, working through API behavior, testing ideas with customers and coordinating integrations across more than a dozen teams.

Run planning→Sample→Analysis→Interpretation

The part that looked simple but wasn’t

We wanted one sample identity that could move across the workflow without breaking the systems customers already depended on. That meant the ideal model had to meet years of legacy behavior somewhere in the middle.

Someone from another team once told me I was working on one of the hardest and longest standing problems at Illumina. I still think about that.

03

Cloud platform

Deleting a file sounds easy.

It was not.

I worked across the infrastructure behind an AWS based data platform. The problems ranged from secure customer storage access to data movement costs and recoverable deletion.

I helped shape requirements for moving customer owned storage toward IAM role based access. I also worked through egress metering and pricing, data lifecycle behavior and file integrity.

Some of this work continued after I moved to SIMS, so I am careful about the distinction between what I helped define and what I personally delivered.

request→authorize→object storage→usage event

04

AWS security

Access keys worked. That didn’t mean we should keep them.

Customer-owned storage relied on IAM users and long-lived credentials. I helped define a path toward role-based access that improved the security model without making existing customer configurations unusable.

The product problem was bigger than replacing one authentication mechanism with another. We had to think through cross-account access, customer bucket policies, role assumption, VPC-restricted environments and how existing configurations could migrate without forcing customers to start over.

What I was solving for

A safer future state that still respected backward compatibility. I worked with engineering and security to turn the architecture into product requirements and a migration strategy before ownership later transitioned to another PM.

05

Cloud economics

Who pays when data leaves the cloud?

AWS data movement creates real cost, but our platform did not originally have a customer-facing way to meter that usage. I helped shape the strategy for turning infrastructure cost into understandable product behavior.

I worked through what should count as billable egress, how usage should be attributed when data is shared and how storage events could become usage records that downstream billing systems could aggregate.

The pricing model also had to translate AWS tiering into something customers could understand. The implementation continued after I moved to SIMS, but the product definition and edge cases became one of my favorite examples of how technical architecture and commercialization meet.

06

Data lifecycle

What should “delete” actually mean?

Permanent deletion is easy to describe and surprisingly risky to design. I explored a recoverable model where deleted data could disappear from normal workflows before a later purge.

Deleting one object could affect analyses, projects, shared resources and large collections of dependent data. We had to consider ordering, asynchronous operations, retention periods, recovery and the point at which deletion becomes irreversible.

delete→hide→retain→recover or purge

07

Multi-tenant compute

Fair doesn’t always mean first come, first served.

I contributed to product work for a shared batch-processing platform where different tenants and workloads competed for compute.

I worked through concepts including fair queueing, quality-of-service priorities, round-robin scheduling and token-based resource allocation. It gave me a much deeper understanding of how platform behavior changes when one shared system has to serve many customers at once.
